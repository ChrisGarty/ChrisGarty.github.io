A player receives the ball, looks up, and starts to weigh her options. From the sideline come the suggestions: “Pass!” “Shoot!” “Long!”

Everyone wants to help. But that moment belongs to the player. She needs the opportunity to see the game, choose an action, and experience what happens next.

For the youth teams I coach, my guidance to parents and coaches is simple: **encourage players, celebrate their actions after they happen, and give them space to make their own decisions in the moment.** Then help them reflect when there is time to listen and learn.

## Parents: praise the action after it happens

The timing of our words matters. “Pass!” tries to choose the next action for a player. “Great pass!” celebrates a decision the player has already made.

Here are examples of the shift I would like us to make:

| Avoid calling out | Celebrate after the action |
| --- | --- |
| “Pass!” | “Great pass!” |
| “Shoot!” | “Nice shot!” |
| “Long!” | “Great vision!” when a player spots an opportunity |
| “Tackle!” | “Strong tackle!” |
| “What are you doing?” | Acknowledge something positive, such as “Strong run!” or “Good effort!” |

These are examples, not a script for judging every touch. Praise should fit what actually happened. There is no need to comment on every decision, and it is fine to let a moment pass quietly.

Simple encouragement works too: **“Yeah!” “Good effort!” “Keep it up!”**

We can appreciate effort, ideas, and intent even when the outcome is imperfect. A player might see a useful passing option but underhit the ball. Another might make a strong supporting run without receiving a pass. Those actions can still deserve encouragement.

“What are you doing?” is particularly unhelpful when shouted across the field. It can sound like criticism without giving the player a useful opportunity to answer. If there is something to understand, that conversation needs a different time and tone.

## Coaches: the same principle applies to us

Coaches need to model the behavior we ask of parents. If I ask families to give players room to decide, I should give them that same room.

That takes restraint. We see an open teammate or a chance to shoot and want to call it out. But repeatedly supplying the answer takes away opportunities for players to recognize those moments themselves.

My aim is for the majority of coaching points during a game to happen with players on the sideline, where we can have a brief conversation. Those conversations should begin with a question that helps me understand the player's thinking.

## Start with curiosity

Before telling a player what to change, find out what she saw and what she was trying to do.

Useful opening questions include:

- “What were you seeing when you received the ball?”
- “What were you trying to do with that pass?”
- “How were you deciding where to position yourself?”
- “What made you choose to dribble there?”

Ask one question and give the player time to answer. This should feel like a conversation, with genuine room for an explanation.

A question such as “Why didn't you pass to the open player?” already assumes that the player saw the option and should have chosen it. “What options did you see?” gives us a better starting point.

Her answer might reveal that she did not see the teammate, thought the passing lane was blocked, or was trying something we had encouraged in training. Each explanation calls for a different response.

From there, we can explore an alternative and agree on one thing to try:

> **Coach:** “What were you seeing when you got the ball on the right?”  
> **Player:** “The defender was coming toward me, so I tried to get past her.”  
> **Coach:** “What other options did you notice?”  
> **Player:** “I didn't really look inside.”  
> **Coach:** “Next time, try taking a look inside before the ball arrives. Then decide whether to pass or dribble based on what you see.”

Keep it brief. A player coming off the field may need a drink and a moment to recover before she is ready to talk. One useful observation and one next step are often enough.

## On the field, coach away from the ball

There is still a place for brief coaching points while players are on the field. Choose players who are away from the ball and have enough attention available to take in the message.

For example:

- When the ball is near our forwards, help the backline think about its spacing and position.
- When play is on the left side, give a brief reminder to players on the right about their positioning and readiness for a switch.

A familiar cue such as “Check your spacing” or “Check your shoulder” may be enough. The player should be able to act on it while continuing to watch the game.

Distance from the ball is only part of the judgment. A player making a supporting run or tracking an opponent may still be deeply involved in the current moment. If play moves toward the player you are addressing, stop and let her play. Save anything that needs a longer explanation for the sideline.

The same principle applies to questions: shouting a question at the player on the ball still asks for her attention at the moment she needs it for the game.

## Why this matters

I want players to build the habit of observing, deciding, acting, and reflecting.

During play, they need ownership of the decision. Afterward, guided reflection can help them understand what happened, consider alternatives, and choose something to try next time.

A useful idea will not always produce a successful outcome. An unsuccessful pass does not automatically mean the choice was wrong, just as a completed pass does not tell us everything about the options available. Talking with the player helps us understand both the decision and the execution.

Over time, the aim is for players to begin asking themselves the questions we initially help them explore: What did I see? What happened? What could I try next time?

That is the independence I want our sideline behavior to support. We can bring plenty of enthusiasm to the game while leaving the decisions with the kids.
