Before games, I want to activate three things: the player's body, the player's mind, and their awareness of the playing surface and conditions. My warm-up activities should work together to address all three.

## Why use activities with the ball?

A standard approach for some teams is to use dynamics: stretching and movement in lines, following a predetermined sequence. These can warm the body and help players get into the right mindset. Repeating the same routine before each game can bring players back to a familiar state of readiness. Once learned, that routine may need little or no coach direction.

The limitation is that these activities are largely physical. They do not offer much practice in decision-making or technical skills. Without a ball, players also get limited feedback about how the surface and conditions affect dribbling, passing, receiving, and shooting.

My preference is to use activities that combine those elements. That does not mean starting at full match intensity. I want players to begin at an easy pace and gradually increase the speed and intensity of their movement and play. The aim is progressive preparation, with the ball involved. Players may still need individual preparation alongside the team activities.

The ritual does not have to come from dynamics in lines. Familiar rondos and other repeated activities can also become a pregame routine that players recognize and run with little direction. They can prepare to play by doing things that connect directly to the game.

## Activity 1: Crossing and finishing

This is the activity my teams tend to start with when a goal is available. It appeals to the players' desire to shoot on goal and, once familiar, requires very little coach direction.

There are no opposing outfield defenders. The goalkeeper provides an opponent to consider when choosing the final pass and shot. This makes it a relatively simple way to get moving, handle the ball, and rehearse some attacking ideas.

### Setup

- Set out two central cones near the halfway circle and two wide cones, creating four starting lines.
- Start all the balls with the players at the two central cones.
- Have a goalkeeper in goal, or a coach who can take that role.

### How it works

1. A central player makes a diagonal pass to a wide player.
2. The wide player receives and dribbles down toward the corner.
3. After passing, the central player runs toward the edge of the penalty area, delays slightly, and times a run into the box.
4. The wide player delivers a pass or cross to the arriving player, aiming to keep the ball away from the goalkeeper.
5. Ideally, the arriving player meets the ball between the penalty spot and the six-yard box and finishes on goal.

The goalkeeper gives the players something to read. The wide player needs to think about where to deliver the ball, and the finisher needs to choose where to shoot. I want players looking to finish into the corners rather than simply hitting the ball toward the goal.

### What it activates

The opening pass activates longer passing and the idea of playing wide, around the outside of central midfielders and central defenders. The wide player's movement activates dribbling and playing into the corner. The central player's run brings in timing, anticipation, and arriving to meet a delivery.

The final action adds shooting and shot selection. The goalkeeper also gets opportunities to prepare through the deliveries and shots, with the pace building as players get ready.

The activity gives players feedback about the conditions over a useful stretch of the field:

- Is the grass wet or dry?
- Is the surface smooth or bumpy?
- How does the ball travel on longer passes and during dribbling?
- How does it behave in the area where players will finish?
- What is the ground like around the goal? Are there holes or uneven areas to notice?

Players are preparing physically and mentally while learning how the ball behaves on the surface they are about to play on.

### The coach's role

Once players know the activity, my role can be mostly encouragement. I can act as the goalkeeper or, with a goalkeeper taking part, stand behind or to the side of them toward the back of the goal, retrieving balls from the net and encouraging both the goalkeeper and the outfield players.

Examples include:

- "Good shot."
- "I like how you aimed for the corner."
- "That was a good cross."

If a player misses the ball or fluffs a shot, encourage them to stay with it: get the ball, turn, and try to score. Keep the response positive and encourage another effort.

### When a goal is not available

This activity requires a goal. At tournaments where a goal is not available during warm-ups, skip it or move it later in the warm-up once a goal becomes available.

## Activity 2: Two-team rondo

This is my primary rondo activity before games. It combines close control, short passing, scanning, and decision-making with pressing and the challenge of keeping the ball in play.

### Setup

When the field is available, the ideal area is beside the penalty area: between the side of the 18-yard box and the touchline, and between the end line and an extension of the top of the penalty area.

Use cones to extend the line at the top of the penalty area out to the touchline. Then use another four or five cones to divide that rectangle into two halves. Put four pinnies on the dividing line.

Split the players into two groups, A and B, with one group in each half. The groups do not need separate team colors at the outset; the players entering the other half to press use the pinnies to distinguish themselves. Ideally, the possession group has at least four players, although it can have up to about ten.

If the field is unavailable, any suitable rectangular space that can be divided into two halves will work. The coach simply needs enough cones to mark the area.

### How it works

1. One group starts with the ball in its half and passes among its players.
2. One player from the other group picks up a pinny and enters that half to press.
3. To win possession for their team, the pressing player must win the ball and complete a pass back to their own team's half. Merely touching the ball out does not win possession: if the defender knocks it out, the ball stays with the team that had it.
4. The possession team earns one point for every five passes it completes.
5. Each point allows the opposing team to send in one more pressing player. After five passes, there can be two pressers; after ten passes, three; and so on.
6. When the defending team wins the ball and successfully transfers it to its own half, play moves to that half and the teams exchange possession and pressing roles.

The challenge increases as the possession team succeeds. Players have to keep finding passing options as more opponents enter their space.

### What it activates

- **Close control and short passing:** receiving and moving the ball in a confined area.
- **Scanning and decision-making:** noticing teammates, opponents, and available space before choosing the next action.
- **Pressing:** engaging with the task of winning the ball back, then doing something useful with it.
- **Controlling the middle:** recognizing and competing for the central space and passing routes.
- **Keeping the ball in play:** valuing controlled possession rather than simply knocking the ball away.

Players also get some feedback about the conditions. On wet grass, for example, I may encourage them to use the broad inside surface of the foot for a controlled reception.

That feedback has limits. A small area beside the penalty box may not represent conditions across the whole pitch. It gives players a taste of how the surface and ball are behaving, rather than a complete picture of the field.

### Alternative: Three-team "over the river"

For an occasional variation, divide the players into three teams and mark three areas: two end areas with a middle area, or "river," between them.

When one end team has the ball, the middle team sends one or two players in to press and try to win it. The possession team aims to complete three to five passes before transferring the ball through the middle to the team in the far area.

This variation puts more emphasis on longer passing and progressing the ball. It also needs more space, more players, and more oversight from the coach. It can be messier, with balls traveling farther out of the activity and more spare balls needed to keep play moving.

I use it to bring some freshness to the pregame routine. For my usual warm-up, the two-team rondo is generally the better fit: simpler to organize, with close touches, decisions, and pressing built into the activity. Over the river offers variety when the space and circumstances suit it.

## Activity 3: Three versus two to goal

This activity prepares players physically and mentally for attacking and defending close to goal. It emphasizes central attacking play, complementing the wide attacking play in crossing and finishing. With defenders present, the attackers have to read the opposition and decide whether to shoot, dribble, or pass.

### Setup and player numbers

The core activity needs three attackers and two defenders, with a goalkeeper or a coach in goal. My usual setup is two defenders, six to nine attackers, and one goalkeeper: nine to twelve players in total. With nine attackers, that gives three players in each attacking line and twelve players overall.

- Place three cones roughly halfway between the top of the penalty area and the halfway line: one central, one to the right, and one to the left.
- Have the attackers form a line behind each cone, with all the balls starting at the central cone.
- Start the two defenders inside the penalty area, ideally wearing pinnies.
- Use a goalkeeper in goal, or have the coach take that role if no goalkeeper is available.

Encourage attackers to change starting positions between repetitions. Starting centrally, on the right, and on the left gives them experience in different positions relative to the ball.

### Starting and playing each repetition

The central attacker starts play. They do not have to pass immediately: they can dribble forward, take the available space, and draw a defender before deciding what to do next. This self-directed start works well and keeps the activity moving without needing the coach to feed every ball.

If two coaches are available, one can instead stand near the attacking starting positions and pass a ball into any of the three attackers to start play.

Once the attackers begin playing, the defenders can move outside the penalty area to engage them. The attackers work together to create a scoring opportunity against the two defenders and goalkeeper.

After each repetition, the defenders reset inside the penalty area. This gives the next three attackers space to advance and a fresh opportunity to read the defenders.

### What it activates

The attackers face several connected decisions:

- **Shoot:** Am I close enough, with a realistic chance of scoring? Where can I place the shot?
- **Dribble:** Is there space I can take? Can I advance with the ball and draw a defender?
- **Pass:** Would a teammate have a better opportunity? Which of my two teammates should receive the ball?
- **Weight and placement:** How hard should I pass? Should I play to the teammate's feet or into space ahead of them to help create an easier finish?

The defenders prepare to pressure the ball, provide cover, and make decisions together against an attacking overload. Both groups are responding to opponents rather than simply following a passing sequence.

Because the activity uses a larger area, players also get more exposure to the playing surface and conditions as they advance, pass, defend, and shoot.

### Keeping two defenders or rotating two pairs

My default is to keep two players focused on defending for a block of five to ten repetitions, resetting inside the penalty area each time.

For an 11v11 group with around fourteen or more players, I may use four defenders in two pairs. One pair defends while the other waits near the goalposts, then they switch after each repetition. The waiting players can also help retrieve balls from shots that miss the goal.

Switching pairs can add time between repetitions, but it gives the defenders a break and provides extra help returning balls. I generally use this version when the player numbers justify it.

### What happens when the defenders win the ball?

A simple objective is for the defenders to win possession and pass the ball back up to the next set of attackers. That gives them a useful action after winning it and helps return the ball for the next repetition.

An alternative is to set up counter goals near the attackers' starting positions. The defenders try to score in those after winning possession.

Counterattacking to those goals asks more of the defenders physically and extends the repetition. I tend to reserve it for a longer version of the activity with two pairs of defenders. A fresh pair can then prepare to face the next attackers instead of asking the same two players to finish a counterattack and immediately recover to defend again. Keep successive repetitions clear of one another as the pairs change.

### The coach's role

With one coach, I prefer to be near the goal: beside and behind the goalkeeper, or beside the goal where I can step in to retrieve balls from the net. If there is no goalkeeper, I can play in goal myself.

That position lets me encourage both sides. For the attackers, I want to recognize taking available space, good shooting decisions, and shots placed into the corners. For the defenders, I want to recognize good pressure, covering a teammate, and sound decisions.

Keep the encouragement positive and let players make the decisions. Praise what they have done, then give the next group the opportunity to read and solve the situation for themselves.

## Choosing activities for the time and space available

With around 45 minutes available for the warm-up and access to a goal, my preferred sequence is all three activities:

1. **Crossing and finishing:** start with longer passing and shooting, unopposed by outfield defenders. This provides an initial emphasis on physical activation while still involving ball work and decisions about the delivery, timing, and finish.
2. **Two-team rondo:** move into close control, short passing, scanning, and pressing with opponents involved.
3. **Three versus two to goal:** finish with attacking and defending decisions close to goal.

With less time, I start with the two-team rondo and then move into three versus two to goal once a goal becomes available.

When time is extremely limited, or no goal is available, the two-team rondo alone is fine. If a goal becomes available and there is a little time left, I may add a very brief spell of three versus two or simply a few quick shots at goal for the attacking players.

The aim is to choose what fits the available time and facilities while preparing the players' bodies and minds for the game.
